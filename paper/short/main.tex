\begin{abstract}
Evaluating thousands of tokens of generated text with an LLM judge that
reads one window at a time is common practice. We audit one such
evaluation, a study of interventions on long, open-ended generation by base
language models \citep{ono2026interrupting}, in which Claude Opus~5 judged
96- to 160-token windows with 600 tokens of context, and report three
problems, each large enough to change its conclusions. (1)~\emph{Provenance}:
sentences the experimenter injected into the stream fell inside the judged
windows and were scored as the model's own; the judge could read them, but
nothing told it who wrote them. (2)~\emph{The horizon}: given the same four
injected sentences in rotation, the model replays its own earlier segments;
at an injection period of 300 tokens, 65\% of judged windows were more than
half verbatim replay and the source of 62\% lay outside the judge's context,
so replay was scored as surprise and as connection. (3)~\emph{Composition}:
read as whole documents, no condition built an integrated text, and the
condition with the best windows read as a sequence of restarts. Under a
corrected protocol, the effect on surprise survives: at period 150 close to
its first-reported size (+1.4 first reported, +1.2 on fresh windows), while the
larger effect first reported at period 300 (+2.7) came from the injected
text (+1.3 on generated text). The effect on connection was mostly replay
(+2.1 first reported, +0.7 on fresh windows). The fresh-only estimates are
stable across shingle lengths from 8 to 16 tokens and copy thresholds from
0.3 to 0.7. We present these as failure modes that long-form evaluations
with windowed judges should test for.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setting and terms}

\citet{ono2026interrupting} asks where the novelty a base language model
produces with no task comes from. Its unit is the \emph{cell}: one premise
(a single opening sentence) continued by a base model, mainly
Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base (8-bit, MLX), for 4,500 tokens with the end-of-text token
masked. An \emph{arm} is a condition applied to every cell; ten premises are
shared by all arms, and 24 arms were run. The terms used below:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1em,itemsep=0.1em,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[Habituation:] a repetition penalty over a moving window (the
log-probability of every token seen in the last 512 generated tokens is
lowered in proportion to its count), which removes literal loops.
\item[Interruption:] a short sentence injected into the model's context as
if the model had written it, introducing a new subject (for example
\emph{``Meanwhile, in a city with no name,''}). The \emph{period} is the
number of generated tokens between injections (150 to 900). The main arms
rotate a fixed set of four such sentences.
\item[Sham break:] an injection without a new subject (a bare paragraph
break, or a connective that asks for continuity), a control for injecting
anything at all.
\item[Reset:] the context is cleared at each interruption, and the stream
resumes from the premise plus the new subject.
\item[Fresh window:] a judged window that is not a verbatim replay of the
stream's own earlier text (Section~\ref{sec:horizon}).
\end{description}

Windows of the stream are judged by Claude Opus~5 on three scales from 0 to
10 (surprise given the earlier text, connection to it, coherence), five
calls per window with the median as the score, with the 600 preceding tokens
shown as context. The first version of the study, written under this
protocol, reported that at period 150 the interruption raised surprise from
1.7 to 3.1 and connection from 1.1 to 3.2 over habituation alone; that its
yield grew with the length of the thread it broke (5.3 to 5.9 in the
windows after periods of 300 to 900 tokens, against 3.1 after 150); and that
a period of about 300 tokens gave the best stream (surprise 4.35). Each of
these numbers was partly an artefact of what the judge could and could not
see.

\section{Three things a windowed judge could not see}

We order them by where the fault lies. The first is an error of the
evaluation pipeline; the second and third are limits of judging a long
stream by windows.

\subsection{Provenance: an error of the pipeline}

The first protocol cut 160-token windows right before each injection and on
a uniform grid, so a window after an interruption contained the injected
sentence. The judge could read that sentence. What it lacked was
provenance: nothing in the window said that the experimenter, not the
model, had written it, and a sentence that opens a new subject is surprising
given the earlier text by construction. This is a segmentation error of the
evaluation design, not a limit of the judge, and segmenting on provenance
fixes it. Re-judging with windows of generated text only (96 tokens starting
32 tokens after the end of the injected sentence, none crossing an
injection; a uniform grid in uninterrupted arms) kept the direction of every
effect and removed the period story: on surprise, the post-interruption
window scores 3.0 at period 150, 2.9 at 300, 2.9 at 600 and 3.2 at 900, and
every paired difference between periods covers zero. Across 14 conditions,
the condition means under the two protocols correlate at $\rho=+0.81$
(surprise) to $+0.93$ (connection); the sizes differ where an injected
sentence sat in the window.

\subsection{The horizon: replay the judge cannot recognize}
\label{sec:horizon}

A base model given the same four subject-change sentences in rotation
learns the rotation: after \emph{``That night she dreamed of something else
entirely:''} it tends to write what it wrote the last time that sentence
appeared, one rotation earlier. This is the self-reinforcing tendency of
language models to repeat sentences already in their context
\citep{xu2022loop}, now cued by the experimenter. We flag a judged window as
\emph{copied} when at least half of its 12-token shingles occur earlier in
the same stream, and record whether the source lies within the 600 tokens
the judge sees (Table~\ref{tab:copy}). Bare generation is 67\% copied
(literal loops, all in view, which the judge scores 0.0), habituation alone
27\%, and the interruption arms 72\% at period 150 and 65\% at 300.

Where the source lies depends on the period. At 300, the rotation repeats
every 1,200 tokens and the source of 62\% of all windows lies outside the
judge's context: the judge cannot tell replay from new text and scores it as
surprise and as connection. At 150, the rotation repeats every 600 tokens,
so the source sits at the edge of the horizon and is partly in view; there
the replay mainly inflates connection, because a verbatim reproduction of
the earlier text is, literally, connected to it. At 600 and 900 copying falls
to 38\% and 12\%, and the arm whose context is reset at each interruption
has none. Removing the copied windows changes the interruption's effect on
surprise little and cuts its effect on connection by a third at period 300
and by more than two thirds at 150 (Table~\ref{tab:reconcile}). We found this because a
document-level judge (Section~\ref{sec:compose}) remarked that the stream
``repeats the same four-part block verbatim''.

\subsection{Composition: local gains do not add up}
\label{sec:compose}

Every score above is local. The judge also read the whole 4,500-token
stream of each cell, injected sentences removed, and scored integration
(parts taken up and joined later), development (something builds rather
than restarts), coherence and surprise, once per document ($k=3$). No
condition exceeds 2.5 on any dimension. The interrupted stream over the
preserved context, whose windows are the best, reads \emph{below} the
uninterrupted habituated one (surprise 0.1 against 1.4, $p=0.002$;
development 0.3 against 1.1): a sequence of restarts. The reset arm reads
best (2.0 / 1.6 / 2.2 / 2.5), because each of its parts is a clean piece that
returns to the premise; this replicated on ten new premises.

\section{The protocol that corrects them}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{Windows segmented on provenance}: generated text only,
starting a margin after any injected text and never crossing an injection.
The context shown to the judge may contain injected text; the graded region
never does.
\item \textbf{A self-copy flag and fresh-only estimates}, reported next to
the all-windows ones, with the detector's sensitivity checked
(Appendix~\ref{app:sens}). Across shingle lengths of 8, 12 and 16 tokens and
thresholds of 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7, the fresh-only effect on surprise stays
between $+1.10$ and $+1.29$ (every $p\le0.010$) and on connection between
$+0.68$ and $+1.18$ (every $p\le0.023$), always well below the all-windows
connection effect at period 150 ($+2.37$); copied shares move by at most ten
points. The one detector-dependent quantity is where the source lies at
period 150 (between 5\% and 70\% of windows beyond the horizon), because there
it sits at the horizon's edge; at 300 it is 60\% to 62\% under every setting.
The flag detects verbatim replay only. A design rule follows: never inject
the same sentence twice, or reset the context.
\item \textbf{A document-level reading} of the whole stream, with injected
text removed, as the check that local gains compose.
\item \textbf{The stream, not the window, as the unit of inference}: cell
means, bootstrap intervals over cells, exact sign-flip permutation tests on
paired cells; window-level statistics only as descriptive.
\item \textbf{A measured judge}: the median of five calls; intra-window
spread 0.45 to 0.70; a pipeline test--retest (two arms that turned out
byte-identical in all ten cells were judged independently: medians of five
agree exactly in 91\% of windows on surprise, and cell means differ by
$-0.01$ \ci{-0.09}{+0.07}); and a second judge family, Kimi K2.6, which
agrees at $\rho=0.85$ / $0.77$ / $0.71$ (surprise / connection / coherence)
on a stratified sample of 140 first-protocol windows and at $0.75$ / $0.80$ /
$0.65$ on a random sample of 160 generated-only windows, reproducing the
ordering of conditions in both.
\item \textbf{Human raters, with their reach stated.} Three raters recruited
on a freelance platform, blind to hypotheses and conditions, rated 63
first-protocol windows (nine per condition, stratified by the judge's
surprise score) on surprise and coherence. Their consensus agrees with the
judge at $\rho=0.58$ on surprise ($p=7\times10^{-7}$; single raters 0.40 to
0.50; inter-rater Krippendorff $\alpha=0.30$) and reproduces the ordering of
conditions; on coherence they rank the interrupted arms below the habituated
one, which the judge does not. These data support the ordering of
conditions on surprise and a moderate agreement with the judge. They do not
validate the corrected effect sizes, connection, or the document level,
which rest on LLM judges; a second round on fresh generated-only windows was
designed but not run.
\item \textbf{A pre-registered confirmatory battery} on ten new premises and
a new random seed, analyzed under the protocol fixed in advance: the primary
contrast confirmed ($+1.48$ \ci{+0.70}{+2.07}, one-sided $p=0.003$; on fresh
windows $+0.82$ \ci{+0.03}{+1.44}, $p=0.035$), and the expectation that a
kept context carries connection refuted.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What survives, and what generalizes}

The interruption is real. On fresh generated text it raises judged surprise
over habituation alone by $+1.2$ to $+1.3$ on the original premises and by
$+0.8$ on the confirmatory ones. At period 150 this is close to the size
first reported; the larger effect first reported at period 300, and its growth
with the period, came from the injected sentence. Its effect on connection
was mostly replay. In direction the effect replicates, on generated-only
windows, on three base models from two families, a post-trained model, an
unquantized model and a second genre \citep{ono2026interrupting}. A bare
paragraph break adds nothing detectable, and a connective that asks for
continuity hurts. What did not survive were the claims that depended on what
the judge could not see.

What we think generalizes is narrower than a law and more useful than an
anecdote. When an output does not fit the judge, the evaluation pipeline
decides what the judge sees, and three of its choices can create or erase an
effect. Long-form evaluations with windowed judges should test whether
judged windows contain text the model did not write; whether the model
replays material whose source lies outside the judge's context; and whether
local scores compose into the quality of the whole. We showed each in one
unusual setting, unconstrained 4,500-token base-model continuations with
periodic injections; how often they matter in other long-form evaluations
is an open empirical question.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{LLM judges and their biases.} Strong LLMs are now standard
evaluators \citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval,chiang2023alternative}, and
their biases are well documented: sensitivity to the order of candidates
\citep{wang2023fair} and a preference for their own generations
\citep{panickssery2024selfpref} and for longer outputs
\citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled}. Recent work asks whether a judge tracks
the property it is meant to measure: \citet{chen2026construct} separate a
judge's invariance to construct-preserving edits from its sensitivity to
construct-changing ones and find that high reliability can coexist with low
sensitivity, and \citet{dev2026harness} stress-test judge reliability across
benchmarks. These studies examine how a judge scores an output it sees
whole. Our case concerns a different axis, the unit of judgment: when an
output is longer than the judge's context, the pipeline decides what the
judge sees, and that decision can create or erase an effect.

\paragraph{Evaluating long outputs.} Long outputs are commonly evaluated by
decomposition: into atomic facts \citep{min2023factscore}, into units for
human annotation \citep{krishna2023longeval}, or into chunks whose summaries
are merged, where coherence across chunks is itself a known difficulty
\citep{chang2023booookscore}. A longer judge context is not a full remedy,
since models use long contexts unevenly \citep{liu2023lost}. Local scores
that do not compose into document quality are a judged-quality instance of
the same decomposition problem.

\paragraph{Repetition in generation.} Degenerate repetition under
likelihood-maximizing decoding is long known
\citep{holtzman2020curious,welleck2019unlikelihood}, and sentence-level
repetition reinforces itself: the more often a sentence has appeared in the
context, the more likely it is to be repeated \citep{xu2022loop}. The
self-copy we report is that tendency meeting a fixed rotation of injected
cues, with the copy's source outside the judge's view. For creative text,
\citet{chakrabarty2023artifice} found that no LLM used as an assessor
correlated positively with expert judgments, one more reason to measure the
judge rather than assume it.

\section{Limitations}

The evidence comes from one study: ten premises (plus ten confirmatory
ones), one unusual generation setup, one main judge family with a second
family on samples. Human validation is thin, as item 6 of the protocol
states. The self-copy flag detects verbatim replay only; paraphrased
self-copy would pass as fresh and, if present, would inflate the fresh-only
estimate of connection. The document-level reading uses the same judge
family, with three calls per document.
