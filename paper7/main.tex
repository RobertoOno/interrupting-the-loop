\begin{abstract}
In August and September 2026, an AI assistant (Claude, inside Claude Code) wrote the code, ran the experiments and drafted the
papers of a research program directed by one person. An audit on 5 October found errata in all six of its documents (four
public preprints, one submitted to a workshop, one draft report). We
froze the versions that existed before the audit and replayed, blind, four kinds of check against the \NrefPrimary{} primary
errata: a document-only review by six model families (two runs each), an audit of each paper against a snapshot of the project's
records as they were when it was frozen, a mechanical number check and a citation check. Matching and adjudication were done
by models too, and no step was validated by a person. One fresh review by Claude Opus~5.5
catches \RecOpusAPct--\RecOpusBPct\% of the audited errata (about half of them through findings it rated of low severity),
among them the five numerical inconsistencies a human workshop reviewer had found, and \PrecOpusA--\PrecOpusB{} of its high- and
medium-severity findings are real errors: about half matched audited errata, and most of the others are new errors that two
adjudicating models, Opus~5.5 itself and GPT-6.1 Sol, both confirmed. The twelve reviews together catch \RecConeAllPct\%, and all checks together \RecAllPct\%. What the checks miss is mostly what the text alone cannot
show: in a post hoc coding of each erratum's status on the day of its freeze, the twelve reviews caught \RecConeDocPct\% of the
errata decidable from the document and \RecConeRecPct\% of those that needed the records of the time, and the records-level audit,
in one budget-capped run per document, \RecCtwoRecPct\%. Claims about the work itself (that an AI assistant did the work, that a
count was registered, what a companion paper now says) are mostly of the second kind. A strict post hoc re-check of the matching,
by two blind model judges, keeps \StrictKeptPct\% of the matcher's credits (an erratum credited to a check); under it one Opus
review catches \StrictRecOpusRange\% of the audited errata and \StrictJudgeOpusA{} of the human reviewer's five, and the twelve
reviews \StrictRecConeDocPct\% and \StrictRecConeRecPct\% of the two kinds. The replay also found about \NnewDedup{} errors the
audit had missed, and Chao's capture--recapture estimator, best read here as indicative, puts the total at about \ChaoDedupN{} to
\ChaoFamDedupN{} errors, depending on how the occasions are counted. Pilots on one synthetic study, in four presentations of up to
\PilotTokLargeGpt{}k tokens (the largest with GPT-6.1 Sol only), then tested the writing step: given the whole record, Claude
Opus~5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol between them reconciled a stale draft with the final state in \PilotPapers{} papers without an error in the
twelve claims scored in each, by an unvalidated automated scorer. These pilots, a floor result with later models, cannot show why our
errors happened. They are consistent with the reading the assistant recorded in the notebook after them, on 6 October, from its
dated entries of August and September: the errors came from the process, with papers frozen before the record settled, intentions
written as facts and no reconciliation pass before deposit; the notebook itself also carried some of the errors. Three working
rules follow.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Research agents now write code, run experiments and draft papers, and the bottleneck has moved from producing research to checking
it: their claims are harder to verify than their code is to run \citep{ding2026verification}. Published AI papers already carry
several objective mistakes each \citep{bianchi2025err}; AI-written papers add hallucinated numbers, references and experiments
\citep{beel2025sakana,miyai2025jr,bianchi2025agents4science,miyai2026paperrecon}; and checkers, human or automated, catch a minority
of known errors in most evaluations \citep{schroter2008errors,son2025spot,xi2025flaws,baumgartner2026scicoqa}, though recent agentic
reviewers catch more of the errors injected into papers on purpose \citep{nguyen2026agenticreview}. What is still missing is an account,
on a real program, of which errors reach publication and which checks would have caught them. The case studies of human-directed
agents that exist do not replay checks on their errors or measure what each would have caught
\citep{alzahrani2026persistent,nguyen2026physics,schwartz2026vibe}; the closest logs the errors an agent introduced in one program
and how each was caught, and leaves unknown how many no check caught \citep{orden2026wrongclaim}.

This report gives that account for one program. From August 2026, an AI assistant executed a research program on creativity and
search in language models under the direction of one person, the author. The six documents studied here, frozen between 19 August
and 27 September, are four preprints, a report and a short workshop paper
\citep{ono2026paper1,ono2026paper2,ono2026paper3,ono2026paper4,ono2026paper6,ono2026short}; a fifth preprint, a synthesis, was
audited only for errors inherited from papers~1 and~2 and is not part of the corpus.
The program used pre-registration, single reads and death criteria, and kept a dated notebook and its code, documents and
analyses under version control (the raw run data lived outside the repository). On 5 October an audit of the six documents, again done with the assistant, found errata in every one. We use
that record to ask three questions: which kinds of error got through; how much each kind of check, replayed blind on the versions
frozen before the audit, would have caught; and why the errors happened.

The answers, in short. A single fresh review by a strong model catches about a third of the audited errata, including all
\Njudge{} inconsistencies a human reviewer had found by the matcher's credits (\StrictJudgeOpusA{} under a strict re-check), and almost all of its high- and medium-severity findings were confirmed as real errors,
by an adjudication in which the same model was one of two judges. Most of what the checks miss is what the text alone cannot show, and claims about the work itself are mostly of that kind.
And in the pilots of Section~\ref{sec:why}, eleven papers written from one synthetic study, two current models given the record
made none of these errors when they updated a draft. This cannot show why our errors happened, but it is consistent with the reading the assistant recorded in the notebook
after the pilots, which places them in when and how the papers were written and frozen rather than in the writing itself.

\section{The program and its record}

The assistant wrote and ran all code, operated the data collection and drafted every paper; the author designed, decided and
read. Per the commit trailers that carry one, the assistant models were mainly Claude Fable~5 (August) and Claude Fable~5.1
(September), with Claude Opus~5 for part of the draft report. Papers were written while batteries were still running and were frozen
for deposit soon after the last read. Five rounds of review of one paper and two of another were done by AI reviewer agents; one
human colleague reviewed the drafts of papers~1 and~2; and two anonymous reviewers of a NeurIPS 2026 workshop reviewed the short paper,
which was rejected for capacity with scores of 7 and 6.

The audit of 5 October read each paper against the notebook, the appendices and the public releases, and checked the data for the
items that changed a central claim. It found errata in all six documents. Some changed a declared finding: a claim that no arm
produced a candidate beyond a classic baseline was false in the replication, and a diversity effect said to hold everywhere held in
eight of nine problems. Others ran across papers: data-availability statements were false in four papers, and reviews by AI agents
were called ``independent review'' in one paper and left undescribed in another. All documents were corrected and deposited on Zenodo the same day
\citep{ono2026paper1,ono2026paper2,ono2026paper3,ono2026paper4,ono2026paper6,ono2026short}; the corrected papers~1--3 were
submitted to arXiv as replacements on 22 October.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The study was pre-registered (pre-registration v1) before any replayed check saw the frozen documents; six operational
amendments followed, each with its reason (Section~\ref{sec:avail}), and when each was written is said below. Version~1 had already changed the design of the draft version~0 in nine
points that came from the implementation, before any model call on project text: among them the records-level audit, cut from two models with 60
tool calls and 250 thousand tokens each to one model with 40 calls and two million input tokens; a tenth class, METHOD; and the
exclusion of errata that only appear in the compiled PDF. The taxonomy was written after the audits had been read. Amendments 1 and 3 were committed before the steps they changed.
Amendment 2 was written as the replay began, which started while the matcher's validation set was still being generated; only
the status, counts and cost of its cells were looked at until the matcher passed. Amendments 4--6 were written as or after their
steps ran and were committed together with the results; with amendments 5 and 6 the matching script changed after the hash recorded
at amendment 3; its final SHA-256 begins \texttt{\JudgingHashFinal}, and the deposit's README gives it whole.

\paragraph{Corpus.} The six versions as they stood before the audit: paper~1 v1, paper~2 v1, paper~3 v3 and paper~4 v3, which
were public, the short paper as submitted to the workshop, and the report's draft 0.4, not yet deposited. Each was exported from its git commit, flattened, with LaTeX
comments blanked so a checker sees the text a reader of the PDF sees (not the figures or the rendered bibliography), and with
its bibliography file appended.

\paragraph{Reference set.} The errata of the 5 October audit and the five inconsistencies of the workshop reviewer, one item per
defective statement, anchored by file and line in the frozen version (\Nomission{} items are omissions, with no line): \NrefAll{} items, of which \NrefPrimary{} enter the primary
analysis (\NrefLowConf{} that the audit itself flagged as of low confidence and \NrefPdfOnly{} that only appear in the compiled PDF are
kept out). Three minor items of paper~3 stay in, although the audit headed them ``Menores (baixa)'' (minor, low) on the scale it
applied as confidence; when the set was fixed, before the replay, we read that label as their importance. Without them
(\NrefNoLowThree{} items), one Opus review catches \RecOpusANoLowPct\% and \RecOpusBNoLowPct\%, the twelve reviews
\RecConeNoLowPct\% and all checks \RecAllNoLowPct\%. The audit judged
each item against the record of 5 October; whether each was already wrong on the day of its freeze was coded post hoc, during the
review of this report (Section~\ref{sec:freeze}). Each item has one of ten
classes (Table~\ref{tab:class}) and a severity: G1 changes a central or declared finding (\NsevGOne{} items), G2 changes a
secondary claim or a number by more than rounding (\NsevGTwo), G3 is minor (\NsevGThree). The coding is by the assistant, the
model family that wrote and audited the papers; a second coder from another family (GPT-6.1 Sol) agreed on the class in
\AgreeClass\% of items ($\kappa=\KappaClass$) but only in \AgreeSev\% on severity ($\kappa=\KappaSev$), so severity results carry
that caveat; as registered, the second coder's classes and severities enter a sensitivity analysis (Sections~\ref{sec:class}
and~\ref{sec:preds}).

\paragraph{Checks replayed.} No checker saw the reference set, the audit or any later version, and no model checker had web access;
the citation check queried the arXiv and Crossref services.
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{C1, document-only review}: a fixed prompt, written after the audit (its categories chosen with the audit's findings known, which if anything
favours the checks), that names the kinds of error without
naming any erratum, asking for every error (numbers inconsistent with tables or
appendix, unsupported or overbroad claims, contradictions, false statements about the work's status, data or review, citation
problems), answered by six families: Claude Opus~5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, Kimi~K3, DeepSeek~V3.2, Mistral Large~3 and
Qwen3-Next-80B, two runs each, on Amazon Bedrock, at each provider's default sampling settings; the two runs are
independent calls with the same prompt. The Claude calls used adaptive extended thinking and prompt caching; the other families ran
with their default reasoning behaviour.
\item \textbf{C2, records-level audit}: the same prompt plus read-only tools (list, read, search) over a snapshot of the
repository at the version's commit, without reviews, audits, strategy, funding, field notes or publishing files (the release recipe among them) or
the human rating packs, scrubbed of personal data (so an excluded file looked absent to it); Claude Opus~5.5, one run per document, at
most 40 tool calls and two million input tokens counted cumulatively, cache reads included. Of the six audits, \CtwoForced{} reached
the token cap after \CtwoForcedCallsLo{} to \CtwoForcedCallsHi{} calls and were asked to answer from what they had read; since every
call re-reads the context, they had read at most about \CtwoDistinctLo{} to \CtwoDistinctHi{} thousand distinct tokens each (uncached
input plus what was written to the cache).
\item \textbf{C3, mechanical numbers}: every decimal in the body looked up in the document's own tables and appendix (the
draft pre-registration also named the repository's appendix files; the frozen script, registered by its hash, looks only inside the
document).
\item \textbf{C4, citations}: every bibliography entry with an arXiv identifier or DOI checked for title, author surnames and
primary category. It ran on all six documents, partially on \CfourPartial{} of them (some queries met rate limits or service errors), and \CfourNotReturned{} of
its \CfourFindings{} findings, all counted in its precision, are identifiers the arXiv API did not return.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Matching and adjudication.} Each finding was matched to the reference set by a model outside the checkers (Claude
Sonnet~5.5) with a fixed rubric: same statement and same defect, at any of the erratum's locations and whatever correction the finding
proposed. The matcher was validated before use on \ValPos{} synthetic
paraphrases of known errata and \ValNeg{} synthetic findings raising a minor, different concern about the same statement (recall
\ValRecall, precision \ValPrecision); these were easier than the near misses the draft pre-registration described (the same passage
with another defect, or the same defect elsewhere), and the validation did not test a substantive different concern about the same
sentence, the confusion the strict re-check below found. Of \HandN{} real matches described at the time as drawn at random (the sample is archived; how it was drawn was not
recorded), \HandCorrect{} were right when the assistant (Claude Opus~5.5) checked them one by one; no person checked the matches. Of the \NreplyCone{} C1 replies,
\NparseRecovered{} could not be read as strict JSON, all from the three weakest families: \NparseLenient{} were read by a format-only parser fixed in an
amendment before matching, and the other \NfreeText{} were matched as free text. The matcher left \NmatchUndecided{} findings without a decision;
amendment 6, written after it had run, settled them from the format of its replies: \NmatchRecovered{} were recovered by re-reading
them, \NmatchOmitted{} left out of complete replies were read as not matching (none from the Opus reviews or the records-level
audit), and \NmatchFilled{} were matched in one more pass. Of the \Nfindings{} findings, the \NunmatchedHM{} unmatched ones that
their checker rated high or medium were grouped into \Nclusters{} candidate errors and judged by two models (Claude Opus~5.5 and
GPT-6.1 Sol) reading the document; a candidate counts as a new error when both say yes. Each answered yes, no or uncertain; they gave the same answer on
\AdjAgree\% of candidates (\AdjBothNo{} both no, \Nnew{} both yes, \AdjBothUnc{} both uncertain), Opus said yes to \AdjOpusYes{} and
GPT-6.1 Sol to \AdjGptYes, and \AdjEitherYes{} had at least one yes; the class and severity of a new error are the Opus
adjudicator's. After a referee in the review of this report read some credited findings as raising a different concern about the
same sentence (Appendix~\ref{app:review}), every match to a primary erratum (\StrictPairs{} pairs of a finding and an erratum) was judged again, each pair on
its own and with the matcher's rubric (in one call per document for one judge, in one session for the other), by GPT-6.1 Sol and a fresh Claude Fable~5.1 agent, neither told which check produced a finding (though a mechanical
check's findings show it in their form) and both blind to the freeze coding
and each other. Under this strict credit a check keeps an erratum only if both judges say one of its findings identifies it.
GPT-6.1 Sol accepted \StrictYesGpt{} pairs and the Fable agent, which by its own account read release statements leniently,
\StrictYesFable; they agreed on \StrictJudgeAgree\%, and every pair GPT-6.1 Sol accepted the Fable agent accepted too, so strict credit is
GPT-6.1 Sol's verdict. It keeps \StrictKept{} of the matcher's \StrictCredits{} credits (an
erratum credited to a check), \StrictKeptPct\%. The re-check
is post hoc, written down before the judges ran; the matcher's credits, with amendment 6, remain the primary result.

\paragraph{Analysis.} Recall of each check on the primary reference set by class and severity, precision on high and medium
findings, and Chao's lower-bound estimator \citep{chao1987} for the total number of errors, from which the number no occasion
saw follows. Five predictions and a rule
for continuing to a prospective study were registered. Recall by document for every check, the overlap between checks and the
estimate by class, also registered, were left out of earlier drafts of this report and are in Appendix~\ref{app:registered}. The replay cost US\$\CostRone{} at upper-bound per-token rates.

Table~\ref{tab:recall} gives the main result. A single review by the strongest family catches about a third of the audited errata,
and nearly all of its high- and medium-severity findings are real errors (Wilson 95\%: \PrecOpusALo--\PrecOpusAHi{} and
\PrecOpusBLo--\PrecOpusBHi): about half of them matched audited errata
(\PrecOpusAMatched{} of \PrecOpusAN{} and \PrecOpusBMatched{} of \PrecOpusBN), and most of the others were new errors that both
adjudicators confirmed (\PrecOpusANew{} and \PrecOpusBNew), one of the adjudicators being Opus~5.5 itself. Recall and precision, though, count
different findings: recall counts a finding of any severity, precision only those the checker rated high or medium. Of the
\RecOpusA{} and \RecOpusB{} errata the two Opus runs caught, \RecLowOnlyOpusA{} and \RecLowOnlyOpusB{} were caught only through
findings it rated low, which were never adjudicated (\NlowMatchedOpusA{} of its \NlowOpusA{} and \NlowMatchedOpusB{} of its
\NlowOpusB{} low findings matched an erratum); a reader who acted only on its high- and medium-severity findings would have caught
\RecHmOpusA{} and \RecHmOpusB{}, and over all its findings precision is at least \PrecAllOpusA{} and \PrecAllOpusB{}. The
records-level audit caught \RecLowOnlyCtwo{} of its \RecCtwo{} only through low findings. Two more families reach \MidFamLoPct--\MidFamHiPct\% per run, with lower
precision; three catch few errata, with low precision, and \NparseRecoveredWeak{} of their \NreplyWeak{} replies could not be read as
strict JSON (Section~\ref{sec:method}). The records-level audit, with the same model and the repository of the time (notebook,
appendices and scripts, not the raw run data), catches no more in total than a document-only review, but a different part: \CtwoOnlyVsOpus{} items that
neither Opus review caught, while it missed \OpusOnlyVsCtwo{} that they did. Its precision counts one finding, the only one we found, that the snapshot's
exclusions produced: the audit reported as missing the review reports that a paper said were archived, which had been left out of
its snapshot (\PrecCtwoNoExcl{} without that finding).
Pooling every review and check reaches \RecAllPct\%. Under the strict re-check the two Opus runs catch \StrictRecOpusA{}
and \StrictRecOpusB, the twelve reviews \StrictRecCone{} (\StrictRecConePct\%), the records-level audit \StrictRecCtwo{} and all checks
\StrictRecAll{} (\StrictRecAllPct\%).
With \NrefPrimary{} items, one run's recall carries a wide interval (Opus: \RecOpusALo--\RecOpusAHi{} and \RecOpusBLo--\RecOpusBHi,
Wilson 95\%); on the same errata the two runs disagree on \OpusOnlyA{} items one way and \OpusOnlyB{} the other (exact McNemar
$p=\McNemarOpusP$), so no difference between them is detectable at this size. Recall varies by document: the two Opus runs together caught \RecOpusEitherPshort{} of the \NprimDocPshort{} errata in the short paper
(\SizeKcPshort{}k characters as sent) but \RecOpusEitherPone{} of \NprimDocPone{} in paper~1 (\SizeKcPone{}k) and
\RecOpusEitherPsix{} of \NprimDocPsix{} in the draft report (\SizeKcPsix{}k).

Over the \ChaoS{} errors of the primary set and the new ones, counted across all occasions (the audit, the workshop reviewer and
every replayed check), \ChaoFone{} were seen by exactly one occasion and \ChaoFtwo{} by exactly two. Chao's estimator of a lower bound
gives \ChaoNexact{} errors (the 1987 form; with the factor $(t-1)/t$ for $t=\ChaoT$ occasions, \ChaoNt), so by this estimate all of
us together saw about \ChaoCover\% of them, and about \ChaoUnseen{} remain unseen. The capture histories are not
alike: an audited erratum is
caught by a matched finding of any severity, a new error only by a finding its checker rated high or medium, since low findings were
never adjudicated, which adds to the new errors seen once. Chao's variance
formula, which treats the occasions as independent, gives an approximate 95\% interval of \ChaoLo--\ChaoHi; the estimate is not a
guaranteed bound. The occasions are not exchangeable: the audit defined the primary set, and over the replayed checks
alone, which saw \ChaoReplayedS{} of the \ChaoS{} errors known, the same estimator gives \ChaoReplayedN{}, fewer than are already
known: it counts only errors those checks could catch, and the \ChaoOnlyHist{} seen only by the audit or the workshop reviewer may lie
outside their reach. With occasions this dependent, all of these estimates are better read as indicative than as bounds. The
pre-registration's text defined the occasions per family (the audit, each model family of C1 with its two runs together, C2, C3 and
C4: $t=\ChaoFamT$; another of its passages adds the workshop reviewer), while the frozen analysis script counted each run and the
workshop reviewer; we did not notice the difference until the fifth review round. Per family (the audit as an occasion holding the \ChaoAuditN{}
primary errata it found, and the workshop reviewer's five counting only where a replayed check caught them), \ChaoFamFone{} errors
were seen once
and \ChaoFamFtwo{} twice, and the estimate is \ChaoFamNexact{} (interval \ChaoFamLo--\ChaoFamHi; coverage \ChaoFamCover\%, about
\ChaoFamUnseen{} unseen), or \ChaoFamHNexact{} with the workshop reviewer as an eleventh occasion. All of them estimate a
lower bound, and counting per family gives the larger. They rest on the matcher's credits; with the strict ones, per run, the
estimate is \StrictChaoNexact. With the duplicate above merged, they are \ChaoDedupNexact{} per run and \ChaoFamDedupNexact{} per
family.

\subsection{A second opinion}

Is it better to run the strongest reviewer twice, or once together with another family? The comparison below is descriptive; with
\NrefPrimary{} items no difference is claimed. On the primary errata, two Opus runs
catch \SecSame; one Opus run with one run of another family catches \SecCross{} on average, \SecCrossGpt{} with GPT-6.1 Sol,
\SecCrossKimi{} with Kimi~K3 and \SecCrossWeakLo--\SecCrossWeakHi{} with the three weaker families, so Opus with GPT-6.1 Sol catches
slightly more than two Opus runs, while the average over families catches fewer. Counting the new errors too, the average flips (\SecSameU{} against \SecCrossU), driven by GPT-6.1 Sol (\SecCrossGptU), whose own findings it helped to

the gain could not be separated from the second reviewer's own strength.

\section{What the record says about why}
\label{sec:why}

One reading of these results is that the writing step fails: a model updating a draft copies a superseded value or keeps a stale
status. Models do bind stale values in long, changing contexts \citep{wang2025interference,guo2026update}. Before testing writing
rules on that premise, we piloted it on synthetic study records built with the traps that caught us: a draft written from a first
read, a notebook with superseded runs, an analysis announced and cancelled, a release that left out the individual judgments,
reviews by AI agents, a companion paper corrected later, a model run that differed from the plan, and a hypothesis registered
after the data. Each record has twelve required claims with known truth, scored by Kimi~K3. The pilots were run before the
writing-rules study's protocol was frozen, and it never was; the validation of the scorer that its draft planned (sixty claims
labelled one by one by the assistant, blind to the arm, before any result was read) was not done, so the scores are an unvalidated model's, checked only by the assistant's
reading of three papers.

The writers were Claude Opus~5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol. The tasks were to make the draft consistent with the final record (\PilotPapersFull{} papers), or only to
``update the draft with the final results'' (\PilotPapersNarrow), or, in one paper, to edit the prose while the harness filled the numbers and keyed
claims from the artifacts. The records are one synthetic study, with the same values and the same twelve required claims, in four
presentations: narrated (the truth written in the notebook), at \PilotTokSmallGpt--\PilotTokSmallOpus{}k tokens as counted by the
provider; the same narrated record with distractor entries added, at \PilotTokMidOpus{}k and \PilotTokLargeGpt{}k tokens (Opus~5.5
returned an overload error four times on the largest, which was run with GPT-6.1 Sol only); or
silent: the release contents only in a file listing, the reviewers' nature only in the reviews' headers, the model run only in a
run configuration, and the cancelled analysis only by its absence.

Across \PilotPapers{} papers and \PilotFacts{} required claims (the same twelve in each paper) there were \PilotErrors{} errors and \PilotOmitted{} omissions. Asked
only to update the results, the writers also corrected the release, review, status, model, registration and companion claims; in
the silent record they inferred from the absence that the analysis never ran, read the release listing, and recognised the
reviewers as AI agents from the headers. The assistant read three of these papers against their scores. With the baseline already at zero errors, the full
writing-rules study would most likely have hit the same floor, and was not run.

The pilots do not show why our errors happened: the papers were written by earlier models (Fable~5 and~5.1, with Opus~5 for part of the
draft report) from a less legible notebook, in Portuguese, larger at most freezes than the records Opus~5.5 completed in the pilots
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}). What they show is that two current models, writing with a legible synthetic record in context, made none of these errors in
\PilotFacts{} required claims (\PilotFactsHarness{} of them in the paper whose numbers the harness filled), and the notebook shows how
the papers were actually written. The pilots are consistent with the reading the assistant recorded in the notebook after them, on 6 October,
which places the errors in the process; the reading is post hoc, and the process facts it names come from the notebook's dated
entries and the audit. One caveat: the record was not always right, and the notebook itself carried
at least two of the errata (on 18 August it called the fresh-text surprise effect about one point smaller, and on 25 August the
diversity effect positive in all nine cells). The process:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item papers were written while batteries were still being re-judged, and frozen before the record settled, so provisional
numbers stayed in the body while the regenerated appendix carried the final ones;
\item intentions were written as facts (``are released'', ``is in progress'') and never revisited when the release went out
without the judgments or the second human round was dropped;
\item no reconciliation pass against the final record was run before deposit or announcement;
\item companion papers were updated one at a time, so errors propagated between them.
\end{itemize}
The records-level audit saw each frozen version with the repository of its own date. By our coding, \WorkRecSnap{} of the
\NworkClaimsRec{} claims about the work that needed the records could be decided from that snapshot, though it caught only \WorkRecSnapCtwo{} of
them, and \WorkRecNoSnap{} rested on
artifacts it did not have, the contents of a release and the git history; one more, the human round described as in progress, became
false only after the freeze.

\section{Three working rules}

The rules below come from one program and are offered as hypotheses for others to test, not as findings.

\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Do not write an intention as a fact.} ``Will be released'' until the release exists; ``planned'' until the run has
run. A claim about the work's own state is true only when written, so it must be written after the fact.
\item \textbf{Reconcile before every deposit, and again after any change.} Give a strong model the paper and the whole final
record and ask for the version consistent with it; in our pilots, writers given the whole record of one synthetic study made no errors in the
\PilotFacts{} required claims scored (the same twelve in each paper), \PilotFactsFull{} of them under this instruction (automated scoring; the assistant read three of the papers). The pass is only as good as the record, and here the notebook itself carried some
of the errata. Repeat it for every published paper
that a later change touches, companions included.
\item \textbf{Run a fresh review before submitting.} One review by the strongest available model, on the document alone, would
have caught about a third of the audited errata (about half of them only through findings it rated of low severity, so
all of its findings should be read), more than half of those the document itself could show, and all \Njudge{}
inconsistencies the human reviewer found by the matcher's credits (\StrictJudgeOpusA{} under the strict re-check), with almost all of its
high- and medium-severity findings confirmed as real errors, by an adjudication in which the same model was one of two judges. Add a
second strong family if one is available; a weak one adds noise.
\end{enumerate}
Generating every number from the artifacts is the design of data-to-paper \citep{ifargan2024datatopaper}; locking claims to
approved evidence removed fabricated citations in a controlled test, with preliminary evidence for reported results
\citep{jha2026paperpilot}, ResearchLoop admits claims to a paper through a ledger bound to evidence \citep{xia2026researchloop}, and
deterministic checks caught seeded defects \citep{nam2026gates}. This report's numbers are generated from the archived evidence. But construction only passes on what the
artifacts say: it helps where a manifest of the work's state exists and is kept current, which in this program it was not.

\section{Related work}

Errors in published AI papers have been counted with automated checkers \citep{bianchi2025err}; errors in AI-written papers with
reconstruction and audits \citep{miyai2026paperrecon,beel2025sakana,miyai2025jr,bianchi2025agents4science}; and the verification
gap of autonomous research agents has been surveyed \citep{ding2026verification}. Benchmarks of error detection report low recall
for automated reviewers \citep{son2025spot,xi2025flaws,baumgartner2026scicoqa}, though on errors injected into real arXiv papers the
best agentic review system caught 71.6\% and the union of six models 83.3\% \citep{nguyen2026agenticreview}, AI reviewers whose criticisms overlap far more than
human reviewers' do \citep{kim2026reviewers}, a benefit from a strong second model, but not a lightweight one, on planted errors \citep{song2026second}, and, for human reviewers,
about 2.6 to 3 of 9 planted major errors found \citep{schroter2008errors}. Agents overclaim what they did \citep{smyth2026overclaiming}
and assert tool results they never obtained \citep{gurram2026agentprop}. In the automated alignment researchers of \citet{chen2026aar} the agent does not
write its own scores: a separate evaluator produces them; evidence-locked writing removed
fabricated citations in a controlled test \citep{jha2026paperpilot}, ResearchLoop admits claims to a paper through a ledger bound to
evidence \citep{xia2026researchloop}, and deterministic gates catch seeded
defects that a single LLM review misses \citep{nam2026gates}. Human decision gates, together with deterministic data work, cut
critical failures in AI-assisted social science \citep{zhu2026attention}. The single-investigator case studies closest to ours report events and the practices that caught them, without replaying checks
or measuring what each would have caught
\citep{alzahrani2026persistent,nguyen2026physics,schwartz2026vibe}. Closest of all, \citet{orden2026wrongclaim} logs 58 errors an
AI agent introduced in one research program over 51 days, coded by the mechanism that caught each and how far it had travelled:
no single mechanism caught a majority, an audit by another agent of the same family caught the most, and in four the archive
itself carried the error forward, as our notebook did. It counts only errors that were caught, so it cannot say what each check
would have missed or how many no check caught. What this report adds is a census of the errata in six
documents of one program, four of them public, with a dated notebook and its code and texts under version control, tied to a blind
replay of the checks, with an estimate of what everyone
missed and a test of the writing step on synthetic records.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

This is one program, one assistant family and six documents, not a sample of AI-assisted research. The reference set comes from an
audit by the same family that wrote the papers, and the coder, the matcher and one adjudicator are from that family too; the other
families and the capture--recapture estimate soften this without removing it, and the precision of the strongest reviewer rests
in part on new errors that its own model helped to confirm. Severity coding agreed poorly across coders. No step
was validated by a person: the reference set, its coding, the check of the matcher, one adjudicator and the reading of the pilot papers
are model work. The coding of each erratum's status at its freeze is post hoc and by two model agents of the same family as the audit,
the matcher and one adjudicator, with their disagreements settled by the drafting assistant. Document-only reviews were credited with \RecConeRec{}
errata coded as decidable only from the records; the strict re-check keeps \StrictRecConeRec{} of them, so most of those credits came
from a generous matcher, and the re-check's judges are models too, which accepted one credit resting on a check its checker could not
have made. The matcher's recall on real findings was not measured. In the
assistant's check of the matcher, the \HandOpusN{} sampled matches of Opus findings were all right, and \HandOtherCorrect{} of the
\HandOtherN{} of the other checks' findings were. The direct test of the writing step, giving the reconciliation pass the
real frozen drafts with their own snapshots, was not run. Precision is not comparable across checks, since it counts only matched or doubly adjudicated findings, C3 and C4
were never adjudicated, and its denominator is each checker's own severity labelling, which varied from \HmShareMin\% to \HmShareMax\% of a family's
findings rated high or medium (Opus \HmShareOpus\%, GPT-6.1 Sol \HmShareGpt\%). The records-level audit ran once, with a tool and token budget that cut three of its six runs short, and
without the raw data. The synthetic records are one study, the same twelve facts in
four presentations, legible in a way real notebooks are not; their scoring is automated (Kimi~K3), with three papers read by the assistant, the
pilot writers are later models than the ones that wrote the papers, and records beyond \PilotTokLargeGpt{}k tokens as counted by the provider were not tested. Counted with one tokenizer (cl100k),
the largest pilot is \PilotTokLargeCl{}k and our notebook measured \NbTokFirst{}k to \NbTokLast{}k at the six freezes, so the largest
pilot covers its size at \NbFreezesUnderPilot{} of them for GPT-6.1 Sol and at \NbFreezesUnderPilotOpus{} for Opus~5.5 (whose largest
completed record, \PilotTokMidOpus{}k tokens by the provider's count, is \PilotTokLargeOpusCl{}k in cl100k), though not its legibility or its
language (Portuguese). Papers~1--3 were public on arXiv, and the public mirror of the repository carried the notebook to
28 August, before the replay; the checkers had no web access, but whether these texts were in their training data is unknown. The
author directed the work under study, and the assistant that drafted this report is a later model of the family whose errors it
counts.

\section{The review of this report}
\label{app:review}

Before deposit, this report was put through rule~3 in rounds (in the archive, the second round is round~1, and so on). The
first (fresh reviews by Claude Opus~5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol)
found one wrong count, an overstated causal conclusion and several overbroad phrasings. The second (a referee review with an audit
against the records by Claude Fable~5.1, and document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found, among smaller things, five errors
of the kinds this report counts: examples of uncaught errata that had been caught, a check of the matcher described as done by hand
that was the assistant's, a character count given as tokens, drafts called published, and an availability statement that promised
raw requests which are not archived; its questions prompted the post hoc analysis of Section~\ref{sec:freeze}. The third (a new
referee review with an audit against the records by a fresh Claude Fable~5.1 agent, and new document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and
Kimi~K3) found that amendments 4--6 had been written after their steps, that the records-level audit lacked some of the records
the text said it had, that the notebook itself carried some errata, and smaller things. The fourth (a third fresh referee, Claude Fable~5.1, and
new document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found that the closing sentence of Section~5 contradicted Section~\ref{sec:freeze},
that a coding we did ourselves stood undisclosed behind a number in the abstract, and that the frozen paper~1 carried rater passages
to be redacted before deposit, among smaller things. The fifth (a fourth fresh referee, Claude Fable~5.1, and new document reviews
by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found that the capture--recapture occasions departed from the pre-registration's text, that the
registered sensitivity analyses with the second coder were missing, and that the headline precision mixed audited errata with new
errors that the scored model had helped to confirm, among smaller things. The sixth (a fifth fresh referee and new
document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found that what we had called the notebook's account of why the errors happened was
the assistant's own reading, recorded after the pilots, and smaller things, among them a matching amendment the text did not
mention. The seventh (a sixth fresh referee and new document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found a closely related study we had
not cited, three omissions counted among the errata nothing catches that no check had been asked about, an availability sentence
that did not fit the records-level audit, and smaller things. The eighth (a seventh fresh referee and new document reviews by GPT-6.1
Sol and Kimi~K3) found that the matcher had credited document-only reviews with errata through findings that raised a different
concern about the same sentence, which led to the strict re-check of Section~\ref{sec:method}, and that a sentence on the adjudicators' agreement
left out their third answer, among smaller things. The ninth
(an eighth fresh referee and new document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found that the strict re-check had not been applied
to precision, that the pilots' unfrozen protocol and unvalidated scorer were not stated, that the frozen texts come from a private
repository, and a related benchmark we had not cited, among smaller things. The tenth
(a ninth fresh referee and new document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found that recall and precision counted different
findings, about half of a review's catches coming through findings it rated low, among smaller things. The eleventh (a tenth
fresh referee and new document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found that the matcher's validation had used easier negatives
than the pre-registration's draft described, that one more checker response and the agreement between each pair of raters carried
per-rater data to be redacted, and that a sentence on the notebook's size contradicted the size comparison, among smaller things. The twelfth (an
eleventh fresh referee and new document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found that the claim about the human reviewer's five
had not been carried through the strict re-check, and smaller things, among them percentages rounded half to even. The
thirteenth (a twelfth fresh referee and new document reviews by GPT-6.1 Sol and Kimi~K3) found that one of the new errors was an
audited erratum the matcher had missed, that the four pilot records were one study, and that one credit surviving the strict re-check
rested on a check its checker could not have made, among smaller things. The review stopped there, by the author's decision: every
round had found something, the later ones only secondary errors, so this report surely still carries some. A fresh agent then
checked only that round's changes, recomputing every new number, and found three small points of wording and consistency, corrected
here.
